## Multiple choice

### 1. What does a conceptual model primarily describe?

A. The system’s high-level concepts, organization and relationships
B. Only its final colours and fonts
C. Its complete source code and database implementation
D. The number of participants in a usability experiment

Answer: A. The conceptual model explains how the system is organized and operates, without requiring implementation detail.
Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 3–5

### 2. Why challenge the claim that everyone will happily wear special glasses for a new TV?

A. It is an assumption about users that needs evidence
B. Technological novelty guarantees acceptance
C. Users must adapt to every design choice
D. A conceptual model removes the need for research

Answer: A. Conceptualization should expose assumptions and prevent baseless claims about user acceptance.
Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 3–4

### 3. A shopping cart helps users understand collecting items before purchase. What is it doing?

A. Using a familiar metaphor to explain an unfamiliar interaction
B. Proving that all physical cart properties must be copied
C. Measuring pointing time
D. Replacing every need for feedback

Answer: A. Metaphors transfer useful familiar knowledge, but software need not copy all properties of the physical example.

A. Moving through a virtual museum to discover exhibits

### 5. Which set best characterizes direct manipulation?

A. Visible objects, physical actions and rapid reversible changes with immediate feedback

Answer: A. The lecture lists continuous representation, physical actions and rapid reversible actions with immediate feedback.

A. They present choices for recognition instead of requiring exact syntax recall

### 38. For this practice sequence, count 3P + 3K + 1M + 1R. Given P=1.1, K=0.2, M=1.35 and R=0.02 seconds, what is the KLM total?

A. 5.27 seconds
B. 2.67 seconds
C. 4.52 seconds
D. 6.37 seconds

Answer: A. T = 3(1.1) + 3(0.2) + 1.35 + 0.02 = 5.27 seconds. These are supplied practice constants, not a timing table in the lecture.

### 39. Using T = k log2(D/W + 1), with D=300 px, W=100 px and k=0.2 seconds/bit, what is the predicted pointing time?

A. 0.4 seconds
B. 0.6 seconds
C. 0.2 seconds
D. 4 seconds

Answer: A. D/W + 1 = 4; log2(4) = 2; T = 0.2 × 2 = 0.4 seconds. This predicts pointing, not the entire task.

Answer: A. In this model difficulty depends on the distance-to-width ratio; increasing both equally preserves that ratio.

## Short answers — two-line models

### 1. Define a conceptual model and distinguish it from visual styling.

A conceptual model explains the system’s concepts, operations and relationships at a high level.  
Visual styling concerns their presentation, such as layout, colours and typography.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 3–5, 60

### 2. Why can a familiar metaphor help without being copied literally?

A metaphor transfers familiar knowledge to help users understand an unfamiliar interaction.  
Keep useful correspondences, but avoid copying limitations or behaviours that conflict with the task.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 6–7, 58

### 3. Name the four interaction types listed in the lecture.

Instructing issues commands; conversing uses a conversational exchange.  
Manipulating acts on objects; exploring moves through a virtual environment or physical space.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 8

### 4. State the three defining characteristics of direct manipulation.

Continuously represent relevant objects and actions, and use physical actions instead of complex syntax.  
Provide rapid, reversible actions with immediate feedback on the object of interest.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 11

### 5. Why prototype early instead of implementing the first idea fully?

A prototype lets the team explore alternatives and obtain concrete user reactions at low cost.  
Its low investment makes unsuitable ideas easier to discard or revise before implementation.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 21–25

### 6. Give one benefit and one limitation of a low-fidelity sketch.

A rough sketch is quick to change and directs discussion toward high-level concepts.  
It may not reveal realistic interaction feel, visual detail or system response time.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 24–28

### 7. How does a storyboard differ from a single screen sketch?

A single sketch shows one appearance or interface state.  
A storyboard links key frames to show progression through an interaction or scenario.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 25, 29–30

### 8. Distinguish horizontal, vertical and scenario prototypes.

Horizontal prototypes cover a broad interface surface; vertical prototypes explore selected functions in depth.  
Scenario prototypes support a specific scripted use path, with little or no deviation.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 33, 36

### 9. Why are fidelity and functional coverage different dimensions?

Fidelity concerns how closely the representation resembles the intended interaction or appearance.  
Coverage concerns which features or task paths it supports, so polished screens can still be shallow.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 22–23, 32–33

### 10. Distinguish throw-away, incremental and evolutionary prototyping.

Throw-away prototypes inform design and are discarded; incremental work adds tested components to the product.  
Evolutionary prototyping repeatedly refines the prototype itself until it becomes the product.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 34

### 11. Explain PICTIVE and how users interact with it.

PICTIVE builds a collaborative interface using office supplies, sticky notes and plastic overlays.  
People move the pieces to demonstrate interaction and modify the proposed design during the session.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 31

### 12. Explain Wizard of Oz and one limit of its evidence.

A human wizard simulates responses behind a real or mock interface for unimplemented functionality.  
User reactions can inform interaction design, but they do not prove equivalent automated functionality is feasible.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 43–45

### 13. Why can polished prototypes make feedback less useful?

People may focus on small details, hesitate to change the design or assume the prototype is finished.  
Explain its limits and invite feedback on the workflow and alternatives, not just its appearance.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 32

### 14. Distinguish internal from external consistency with examples.

Internal consistency keeps similar operations alike within an app, such as the same Save behaviour.  
External consistency follows relevant conventions across apps or devices, such as familiar editing shortcuts.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 52–54

### 15. Explain simplicity without equating it with hiding every control.

Simplicity removes inessential elements and uses regular patterns to reduce processing effort.  
Retain the labels, controls and feedback needed to make the task understandable and complete.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 56–57

### 16. Give two justified visual-design improvements for a crowded form.

Group related fields with whitespace and alignment so their relationships are easier to scan.  
Use clear hierarchy, readable type and selective colour contrast to emphasize important actions.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 60–72

### 17. What makes participatory design more than an end-of-project user test?

Representative end users actively collaborate throughout design as experts in their work context.  
Their feedback informs repeated revisions; they are not expected to be expert interface designers.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 75–80

### 18. Distinguish heuristic evaluation from a cognitive walkthrough.

Heuristic evaluation inspects a design against usability rules across a broad range of use.  
A cognitive walkthrough follows a task step by step to assess a new user’s exploratory learning.

Lecture 05 · PDF pp. 81, 85, 89–90

### 19. List the four cognitive-walkthrough questions in order.

Will users try to produce the effect, and notice that the correct action is available?  
Will they know the action is right, and understand the feedback after performing it?

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 90

### 20. A user notices Submit but cannot tell whether it completes the task. Diagnose the walkthrough issue.

The user notices the action but cannot connect its meaning to the intended effect: question 3.  
Use a clear action label and supporting context that communicate what submission will accomplish.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 90

### 21. Explain GOMS using a text-deletion task.

Goal: delete a word; operators: pointing, selection and keystrokes; method: select the word then delete.  
Selection rules choose between that method and alternatives such as repeated Backspace.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 93

### 22. Name the six KLM operators listed in the lecture.

K: keystroke; P: pointing; H: homing the hands on a device.  
D: drawing a straight line; M: mental preparation; R: system response.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 94

### 23. Calculate 3P + 3K + M + R using P=1.1, K=0.2, M=1.35 and R=0.02 seconds.

T = 3(1.1) + 3(0.2) + 1.35 + 0.02.  
T = 5.27 seconds for exactly the stated operators and supplied practice timings.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 94

### 24. State the lecture’s Fitts formula and its design implication.

T = k log2(D/W + 1), with D as target distance and W as width along the movement direction.  
Larger targets or shorter distances reduce predicted pointing time when other terms remain fixed.

Lecture 05 · PDF p. 95
