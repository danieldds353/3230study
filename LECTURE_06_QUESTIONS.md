## Multiple choice

### 1. A team evaluates a prototype to find problems for its next iteration. What is the purpose?

A. Formative evaluation
B. Summative evaluation
C. Random assignment
D. Selective coding

Answer: A. Formative evaluation informs changes during design; summative evaluation assesses the finished product.

### 2. Which activity is analytical evaluation rather than an empirical user study?

A. Inspecting an interface against heuristics without recruiting users
B. Observing participants perform tasks
C. Measuring user completion times
D. Interviewing users during a field trial

Answer: A. Analytical methods inspect or predict interaction; empirical evaluation collects evidence from users.

### 3. A researcher teaches every step before measuring first-use learnability. What is the main problem?

A. The guidance changes what is being measured
B. Training always increases sample representativeness

Answer: A. Step-by-step coaching can conceal first-use difficulties; any necessary training should match the study goal and be reported.

### 9. For a positively worded SUS item, a response of 4 contributes how many points before scaling?

### 10. For a negatively worded SUS item, a response of 2 contributes how many points before scaling?

### 11. A participant’s ten converted SUS item scores sum to 30. What is the overall SUS score?

B. 30
C. 12
D. 100

Answer: A. Multiply the converted total by 2.5: 30 × 2.5 = 75 on the 0–100 scale.

### 12. Which interpretation of SUS is sound?

A. A score of 75 is a scaled usability score, not 75% task success
B. A score of 75 proves 75% of tasks succeeded

Answer: A. SUS summarizes questionnaire responses; performance and percentile interpretation require separate evidence or a reference distribution.

## Short answers — two-line models

### 1. Distinguish formative from summative evaluation.

Formative evaluation finds problems during design to guide the next iteration.  
Summative evaluation assesses the success of a finished product at the end of a design cycle.

Lecture 06 · PDF p. 12

### 2. Distinguish analytical from empirical evaluation.

Analytical evaluation inspects or predicts interaction without observing recruited users perform tasks.  
Empirical evaluation collects evidence from users, through usability tests or field studies.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 5, 10–11

### 3. Why should task instructions avoid teaching every action when testing learnability?

Step-by-step guidance conceals whether users can discover the interaction themselves.  
Give a clear task goal and report any help or training so results remain interpretable.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 14–15

### 4. Give one measure each for effectiveness, efficiency and memorability.

Effectiveness: successful task completion; efficiency: time to complete the task.  
Memorability: task performance after a specified period away from the system.

Lecture 06 · PDF p. 16

### 5. What must happen before recording a participant’s face and voice?

Explain participation and the intended recording, and obtain informed consent.  
The lecture requires written, signed consent before making face or voice recordings.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 14, 17

### 6. Why combine task-performance data with comments and post-task ratings?

Performance data show task success, time and errors; comments can explain the observed difficulties.  
Post-task ratings add perceived usability and experience, which may differ from observed performance.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 16–18, 22–23

### 7. Explain how to calculate a standard SUS score.

Odd positive items contribute response minus 1; even negative items contribute 5 minus response.  
Sum the ten converted values and multiply by 2.5 for a score from 0 to 100.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 19–20

### 8. All five positive SUS responses are 4 and all five negative responses are 2. Calculate the score.

Each item contributes 3: positive 4 − 1 and negative 5 − 2.  
The total is 10 × 3 = 30; SUS = 30 × 2.5 = 75.

Lecture 06 · PDF p. 19

### 9. Why does SUS 75 not mean 75% of tasks were completed?

SUS is a scaled questionnaire score summarizing perceived usability, not observed task success.  
Measure completion separately; use a reference distribution if interpreting SUS percentiles.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 19–23

### 10. Identify the independent and dependent variables in a speed comparison of interfaces A and B.

The independent variable is the interface condition: A or B.  
The dependent variable is measured task-completion time for the defined task.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 30–32

### 11. Old-interface users get slow computers and new-interface users get fast ones. Explain the problem and fix.

Hardware performance varies with interface condition, so it could explain the timing difference.  
Use equivalent hardware across conditions or otherwise design the study to separate the two effects.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 28–32

### 12. Compare between-subjects and within-subjects designs.

Between-subjects uses separate participant groups in each condition, avoiding cross-condition order effects.  
Within-subjects uses the same people in all conditions, reducing individual variation but requiring order control.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 35–49

### 13. Explain matched-participants design and its limitation.

Pair participants on relevant characteristics and place members of each pair in different conditions.  
Matching reduces selected individual differences but cannot guarantee equivalence on every factor.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 35, 49

### 14. Everyone uses A before B and is faster with B. How would you address the order effect?

Practice or fatigue could affect the second condition independently of interface quality.  
Counterbalance exposure by assigning some participants to AB and others to BA.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 38–46

### 15. What does a Latin square contribute to a multi-condition study?

It places each condition once in each sequence position across the planned orders.  
This balances position effects with fewer orders than testing every permutation; it does not remove every confound.

Lecture 06 · PDF p. 47

### 16. Why use a field study to investigate appropriation?

Appropriation concerns how people integrate and adapt technology within their real practices and culture.  
Observe natural use and ask contextual questions to reveal adaptations that lab tasks may miss.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 51–53

### 17. Explain why interviews and observation are not restricted to one data type.

Open comments and descriptions provide qualitative evidence about meaning and experience.  
Closed responses and counted or timed actions can provide quantitative evidence from the same methods.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 57–59

### 18. Distinguish nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio scales.

Nominal labels categories; ordinal orders them without guaranteed equal gaps.  
Interval has equal differences and a conventional zero; ratio adds a meaningful true zero.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 62–66

### 19. Why are recorded age brackets ordinal even though exact age can be ratio data?

Age brackets retain an order but replace exact ages with categories.  
The recorded categories do not preserve exact differences or meaningful ratios between individuals.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 64, 66, 69

### 20. For times 2, 2, 3, 4 and 19 seconds, give the mean, median and mode.

Mean = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 seconds; median = the middle value, 3 seconds.  
Mode = 2 seconds; the unusually long time raises the mean more than the median.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 60–61

### 21. The nine-person example shows lower times for A than B. State a careful conclusion.

This sample appears to perform the task more efficiently with A than with B.  
The descriptive result alone does not establish statistical significance or superiority for all users.

Lecture 06 · PDF p. 71

### 22. Define content analysis and inter-rater reliability.

Content analysis systematically assigns excerpts to defined or emergent categories and examines patterns or counts.  
Inter-rater reliability concerns how consistently different coders assign the same material to categories.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 74–77

### 23. What distinguishes a critical incident from a general impression?

A critical incident is a specific, pivotal event illustrating success or failure in the activity.  
Report the observable event and consequence, such as erroneous barcode entry causing a billing dispute.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 78–79

### 24. Distinguish open, axial and selective coding in grounded theory.

Open coding develops categories from data; axial coding relates categories and subcategories.  
Selective coding integrates them around a central category while collection and analysis continue to inform each other.

Lecture 06 · PDF pp. 80–91
